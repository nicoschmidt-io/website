India is rolling out facial recognition in public spaces: at railway stations, in prisons, at the Ram Mandir temple in Ayodhya. Much of the software comes from Herta Security in Barcelona, a company that has also received more than €3 million in EU research funding. In the EU, police use of real-time facial recognition in public has been largely banned since February 2025.

Part of the rollout is paid for by a fund set up to protect women after the 2012 Delhi gang rape. Who the cameras are actually looking for is unclear: the watchlist reportedly holds around a million people, and the criteria are not public. Four EU law scholars told us two of the systems would likely be illegal in the EU. Herta says it follows European data protection principles but can't control what authorities do with its technology.
